Kanto by the Numbers

A statistical look at Gen 1 Pokémon

All 151 Gen 1 Pokémon in Pokédex order, in their original 1996 artwork.

Anyone who grew up with the Game Boy games or the anime subconsciously picked up a few rules about Pokémon. For example, Mewtwo, the rarest Pokémon of all, was also the most feared, so rarity seemed to go hand in hand with power. Brock's Onix towered over Pikachu in Ash's first gym battle, which made size look like strength. Squirtle made blue look like the colour of Water types, and every evolution seemed to make a Pokémon better.

I was curious whether these rules hold up with statistical significance or are simply impressions. Gen 1, the 151 Pokémon of the Kanto region, is well suited to the question, because each one has an official height, weight and colour, a catch rate hidden in the game's code, and six battle stats. The data come from PokéAPI and two public Kaggle datasets (listed at the end), and the analysis was done in Python.

The battle stats are the numbers the games use to decide a battle. HP is how much damage a Pokémon can take before it faints. Attack and Defence govern physical moves such as Tackle, while Special Attack and Special Defence cover special moves such as Thunderbolt. Speed decides who moves first. Added together, the six give a Pokémon's base stat total, which this page uses as its overall strength.

The first five questions below each test one of those rules, and the sixth asks whether a machine learning model can predict a Pokémon's strength without seeing any of its battle stats.

Q1: Are rarer Pokémon stronger?

Every Pokémon has a catch rate, from 255 (a Poké Ball almost always works) down to 3. Only four Pokémon sit at 3, and all of them are legendary (Mewtwo, Articuno, Zapdos and Moltres). If rarity reflects power, the harder a Pokémon is to catch, the stronger it should be.

The standard way to measure whether two things move together is a correlation, a score from −1 to +1. A value near +1 means that as one rises, so does the other; a value near 0 means there is no pattern; and a value near −1 means that as one rises, the other falls. This page uses Spearman's rank correlation, written ρ (rho) and calculated with the SciPy library. It ranks the Pokémon from lowest to highest on each measure and compares the two rankings, which stops a few extremes such as Mewtwo from dominating the result.

Between rarity and strength, ρ = 0.73, a strong positive link (Fig. 1). Mewtwo is both the hardest Pokémon to catch and the strongest of all 151.

With only 151 Pokémon, a pattern can appear by chance, so every result on this page was also checked with a permutation test, written in Python with the NumPy library. The test shuffles one of the two columns at random, which breaks any real connection, and recalculates the correlation. Repeated 10,000 times, this shows the range of results that chance alone can produce. If the real result lies far outside that range, chance is an unlikely explanation. For rarity and strength, none of the 10,000 shuffles came close, and the shuffled correlations never exceeded 0.33, against a real value of 0.73 (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2. Permutation test for catch rate and strength

Comparing each Pokémon's rank for strength with its rank for rarity identifies those that are better or worse value than their catch rate suggests (green and orange in Fig. 1). The three starters, Bulbasaur, Charmander and Squirtle, are among the worst value. They share a catch rate of 45 with many fully evolved Pokémon, yet they are far weaker, which rarely matters because Professor Oak hands one over for free. Their low catch rate is likely explained by what they evolve into. Their evolved forms, Venusaur, Charizard and Blastoise, rank 11th to 13th in strength among all 151. Likewise, Dratini, another poor-value Pokémon, grows into Dragonite, joint second at 600 points. In other words, the catch rate appears to reflect the strength of the whole evolution line rather than that of the Pokémon itself. At the other end, Arcanine, Ninetales, Cloyster, Muk and Golduck are relatively easy to catch but reach 500 points of strength or more.

Q2: Does size equal power?

Heavier Pokémon are stronger, and so are taller ones, with ρ = 0.72 for both. However, evolution could explain this, because evolving makes a Pokémon bigger and stronger at the same time, so size and strength might be linked only because both increase with evolution.

To test this, each evolution stage was analysed separately (Fig. 3). The link remains within every stage, with ρ = 0.71 for first-stage Pokémon, 0.52 for second-stage and 0.73 for third-stage. So the link between size and strength is not just a side effect of evolution.

Fig. 3 plots weight on a logarithmic (log) scale, where each step along the axis is ten times heavier than the last. Gen 1 weights range from 0.1 kg (Gastly) to 460 kg (Snorlax), but 135 of the 151 Pokémon weigh less than 100 kg. On an ordinary scale, the few very heavy Pokémon, such as Snorlax, would stretch the axis and squeeze almost every other Pokémon into one corner of the chart.

Brock's Onix is the clearest exception. At 8.8 metres it is the tallest Pokémon in Gen 1, and at 210 kg one of the heaviest, yet its strength of 385 is lower than that of many Pokémon a fraction of its size.

Q3: Can a Pokémon be both fast and tough?

Toughness here means how much damage a Pokémon can absorb, measured as HP plus Defence plus Special Defence. Across all 151 Pokémon, the correlation between speed and toughness is only ρ = 0.16, and the permutation test cannot rule out chance (Fig. 4).

Within each evolution group, however, faster Pokémon tend to be less tough, a trade-off between speed and toughness. This holds for the 70 Pokémon that are not yet fully evolved (ρ = −0.41) and for the 81 fully evolved Pokémon (ρ = −0.36), and both results pass the permutation test. Snorlax is slow and very tough, Alakazam is fast and fragile, and Mewtwo is the main exception, being both fast and tough.

The trade-off disappears when the two groups are combined because fully evolved Pokémon are faster and tougher overall, with a median speed of 80 against 55 and a median toughness of 235 against 155. On one chart, the fully evolved group sits above and to the right of the other (first panel of Fig. 4), so speed and toughness appear to rise together and the trade-off within each group is hidden, an effect known as Simpson's paradox.

Q4: Does colour give away the type?

The Pokédex, the gadget every young fan wanted, myself included, assigns each Pokémon one official colour. Colour and type are clearly linked. In a permutation test that shuffled the colours 10,000 times, no shuffle produced a link as strong as the real one.

Green Pokémon are seven times more likely than average to be Grass types (Fig. 5). Pink Pokémon lean towards Psychic and Normal, yellow towards Electric and red towards Fire. The rule about blue also holds, with 16 of the 25 blue Pokémon being Water types.

Colour does not determine type, however. Misty's Psyduck is yellow but a Water type, and its evolution, Golduck, is blue. Magikarp is red but a Water type, Voltorb is red but an Electric type, and most red Pokémon are not Fire types at all.

Q5: How much stronger does evolving make a Pokémon?

Gen 1 contains 72 evolutions, and every one of them makes the Pokémon stronger. The typical (median) evolution adds 140 points of strength, roughly 42%.

Magikarp to Gyarados is the largest gain by far, at 170% (Fig. 6). At the other extreme, Caterpie to Metapod is the smallest, at 5%, for a Pokémon that spends its whole life as a cocoon. Across all 72 evolutions, Special Defence and HP grow the most and Speed the least.

The first season of the anime, Indigo League (first aired in Japan from 1997 to 1999), offers several examples. Team Rocket learned about that 170% the hard way. Aboard the St. Anne, James spent the team's money on a Magikarp from a salesman who promised it would breed a fortune. It proved useless until, in the very next episode, it evolved into a Gyarados, learned Dragon Rage and left him.

The same season offers two more test cases. When Ash's Charmeleon evolved into Charizard during the battle with Aerodactyl, it gained 32% in strength, along with a habit of ignoring Ash's orders. Ash's Pikachu, on the other hand, refused to evolve. A Raichu is 52% stronger, yet Pikachu still beat Lt. Surge's Raichu in their rematch by relying on speed rather than power.

Q6: Can a machine learning model predict a Pokémon's strength?

The questions so far examined one relationship at a time. The last question combines them, asking whether a model can use everything that is visible without a battle to predict how strong a Pokémon is.

The model was given seven features, the inputs it learns from. These were height, weight, colour, catch rate, evolution stage, whether the Pokémon is fully evolved, and whether it is legendary or mythical. It never saw any of the six battle stats, because their total is the target, the value it had to predict. Fig. 7 shows the table exactly as the model received it. Before training, height and weight were converted to a log scale, as in Fig. 3, and colour was one-hot encoded into ten 0/1 columns so the model could use it as numbers. Fully evolved and legendary are coded as 1 for yes and 0 for no.


    
    
Fig. 7. The model's input table

  

  

    
The model is a random forest, a bagged ensemble of 500 decision trees built with the scikit-learn library. Each tree learns a sequence of yes-or-no splits, such as “Is it fully evolved?” or “Does it weigh more than 50 kg?”, from its own bootstrap sample of the data, and the forest averages the 500 predictions.

    
A model must be tested on Pokémon it has not seen, otherwise it can simply memorise the answers. The model was therefore evaluated with leave-one-out cross-validation. It was trained on 150 Pokémon and asked to predict the one left out, and this was repeated until every Pokémon had been predicted once.

    
Accuracy is reported as the mean absolute error, the average distance between the predicted and the true strength. The random forest's mean absolute error is 32 points (Fig. 8). For comparison, a baseline that predicts the average strength for every Pokémon is off by 86 points, so the model reduces the error by about 63%. A linear regression, which fits a single weighted sum of the features, performed almost identically. This suggests that with a dataset this small and relationships this simple, the more flexible random forest offers little extra.

The largest errors come from Pokémon whose appearance is misleading. Onix is one of the heaviest Pokémon, so the model expected it to be strong. It is not. Ditto is hard to catch, so the model also expected more of it. Mew is the opposite case, small and light yet one of the strongest Pokémon in the game.

    
To see which features the model relied on, its permutation feature importance was measured by shuffling each feature in turn and recording how much the error increased (Fig. 9).

  

  

    

    
Fig. 9. Feature importance (increase in mean absolute error when each feature is shuffled)

  

  

    
Evolution stage, including whether a Pokémon is fully evolved, matters most by far, followed by size. Colour adds nothing, which is consistent with Q4, since colour hints at type and type says little about strength. Catch rate appears unimportant, even though Q1 found a strong link with strength. The reason is that catch rate and evolution stage carry largely the same information, so once the model knows the stage, the catch rate adds little. More generally, feature importance shows what a model needs given the other features, not how much each feature matters on its own.

  






  

    
What Kanto tells us

    
The data support most of these rules, but each has exceptions. First, rarity goes with strength, and the link is strong. However, catch rate does not reliably predict the strength of an individual Pokémon, as the weak but hard-to-catch starters show. Second, size is linked to strength at every stage of evolution, so the link is not just a side effect of evolution. Third, colour often hints at type, but many Pokémon break the pattern. Finally, evolution is the only rule without an exception, with all 72 evolutions increasing strength.

    
The result on speed and toughness was less expected. Faster Pokémon tend to be less tough both before and after they are fully evolved, yet the trade-off vanishes when all 151 are analysed together. This shows that a result for a whole dataset can hide a pattern within its groups, so the groups are worth checking.

    
Beyond the individual rules, the machine learning model combined these factors. Using only information available without a battle, it predicted strength to within about 32 points on average. The model relied most on evolution stage and size, the same factors identified by the individual questions. One caveat is that, with 151 Pokémon and only five legendary or mythical ones among them, the sample is small, so these findings describe Kanto rather than the whole Pokémon world.

  






  

    
Methods and caveats

    
Data

    
    
      
  • The 151 Pokémon of Generation 1, with Mega Evolutions removed. Battle stats come from a Kaggle dataset. Height, weight, colour, catch rate and evolution links come from PokéAPI.
    • 
      
  • Types are the original 1996 types. Clefairy, Clefable, Jigglypuff, Wigglytuff and Mr. Mime were later changed to Fairy, and Magnemite and Magneton gained Steel. Here they keep their original types.
    • 
      
  • Strength is the base stat total, the sum of the six battle stats (HP, Attack, Defence, Special Attack, Special Defence, Speed), using the values from later games. The original games had a single Special stat.
    • 
      
  • Six Pokémon received small stat boosts in later games. This analysis uses the earlier values, which does not change any conclusion.
    • 
    

    
Statistics

    
    
      
  • Correlations are Spearman rank correlations (ρ), which handle uneven and skewed data such as catch rate and weight.
    • 
      
  • The ranges shown with each ρ are 95% confidence intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples.
    • 
      
  • Permutation tests use 10,000 random shuffles. In Q3, the trade-off holds within both evolution groups but not when they are pooled, an example of Simpson's paradox. Q4 measures the colour–type link with Cramér's V on primary types. Q5 uses a paired sign-flip permutation test on the 72 evolution pairs.
    • 
      
  • Because several tests were run, p-values were also adjusted with the Benjamini–Hochberg method. No conclusion changes after the adjustment.
    • 
      
  • 151 Pokémon is a small sample. Some groups are tiny: there are only 3 Ghost types, 1 black Pokémon and 5 legendary or mythical Pokémon, so treat those details lightly.
    • 
    

    

    
The machine learning model

    
    
      
  • A random forest regression model (500 decision trees, minimum two Pokémon per leaf, scikit-learn) predicted strength from log height, log weight, one-hot colour, catch rate, evolution stage, whether fully evolved, and legendary or mythical status.
    • 
      
  • Leave-one-out cross-validation means every one of the 151 predictions came from a model that never saw that Pokémon. A linear regression model (ridge regression) did almost exactly as well, with a mean absolute error of 32.1 points against 31.5.
    • 
      
  • Permutation feature importance shuffled one group of features at a time across 50 train-and-test splits (5-fold cross-validation, repeated 10 times).
    •
